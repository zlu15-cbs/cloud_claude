Why a just-identified BLP may not pin down the random coefficient on the expense ratio

A walk from the optimization problem to the moment conditions, to the rank deficiency Danny reports, to which instruments would identify the random coefficient.

Hover over, or tap, any dotted-underlined symbol to see its definition. Passages on a yellow ground are the points Danny's note makes.

The short version

  • A just-identified GMM problem is M equations in M unknowns. If those equations have a solution, estimation is regular and the standard errors are valid.
  • If they have no solution, the optimizer stops at a point where the moments are not zero. The Jacobian is always singular at such a point. This is the rank deficiency Danny describes.
  • Whether a solution exists depends on the instruments. The random coefficient needs an instrument that moves fees differently for high-fee and low-fee funds, or that shifts which rivals sit close in fee. An instrument that only shifts the fee level does not identify it.
  • Danny's extra instrument removes the algebraic obstacle. It does not by itself make the random coefficient well identified: for passive funds its standard error is still several hundred times the estimate.

1. The demand model

Investor \(i\) chooses among index funds \(j\) in month \(t\), or the outside option.

\(\displaystyle u_{ijt}\) \(=\) \(\displaystyle \delta_{jt}\) \(+\) \(\displaystyle \sigma\) \(\displaystyle p_{jt}\) \(\displaystyle \nu_i\) \(+\) \(\displaystyle \varepsilon_{ijt}\) (1)
\(\displaystyle \delta_{jt}\) \(=\) \(\displaystyle x_{jt}'\) \(\displaystyle \beta\) \(+\) \(\displaystyle \eta_j\) \(+\) \(\displaystyle \tau_t\) \(+\) \(\displaystyle \xi_{jt}\) (2)

The model's market share integrates the logit choice probability over investors:

\(\displaystyle s_{jt}(\delta_t,\sigma)\) \(=\) \(\displaystyle \int\) \(\displaystyle \frac{\exp(\delta_{jt}+\sigma p_{jt}\nu)}{1+\sum_k \exp(\delta_{kt}+\sigma p_{kt}\nu)}\) \(\displaystyle \phi(\nu)\,d\nu\) (3)

Share inversion. For any trial value of σ there is exactly one vector of mean utilities, \(\delta(\sigma)\), that makes the model's shares match the observed ones. The implied demand shock is then

\(\displaystyle \tilde\xi(\beta,\sigma)\) \(=\) \(\displaystyle \tilde\delta(\sigma)\) \(-\) \(\displaystyle \tilde X\) \(\displaystyle \beta\) (4)

2. The optimization problem

PyBLP chooses the parameters to make the demand shock as uncorrelated with the instruments as possible:

\(\displaystyle \hat\theta\) \(=\ \arg\min_{\theta=(\beta,\sigma)}\) \(\displaystyle Q(\theta)\) \(,\qquad\) \(\displaystyle Q(\theta)\) \(=\) \(\displaystyle N\) \(\displaystyle g(\theta)'\) \(\displaystyle W\) \(\displaystyle g(\theta)\) (5)

The first-order condition is the equation everything below turns on:

\(\displaystyle \nabla_\theta Q\) \(=\ 2N\) \(\displaystyle G'\) \(\displaystyle W\) \(\displaystyle g\) \(=\ 0\) (6)

3. The moment conditions

Population. The identifying assumption is that the instruments are uncorrelated with the true demand shock:

\(\displaystyle \mathbb{E}\big[\) \(\displaystyle z_{jt}\) \(\displaystyle \xi_{jt}(\theta_0)\) \(\big]=0\) (M moments)

Sample. Because β enters linearly, the sample moments split into a part that depends on σ and a part that is linear in β:

\(\displaystyle g(\beta,\sigma)\) \(=\) \(\displaystyle \frac{\tilde Z'\tilde\xi(\beta,\sigma)}{N}\) \(=\) \(\displaystyle b(\sigma)\) \(-\) \(\displaystyle A\) \(\displaystyle \beta\) (7)
\(\displaystyle G\) \(=\ \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial\theta'}\ =\ \big[\) \(\displaystyle -A\) \(,\) \(\displaystyle B(\sigma)\) \(\big]\) (8)

Two conditions.

  • Order condition: at least as many moments as parameters, M ≥ P. With M = P the model is just identified.
  • Rank condition: the Jacobian at the truth has full column rank P. In words: the instruments must detect a change in every parameter, and a change in σ must move the moments in a way that no change in β can copy. Counting instruments does not check this.

4. One random coefficient: the problem collapses to one equation in σ

Take the just-identified case with one random coefficient, M = K + 1. For a given σ, the best β is a linear IV estimate:

\(\displaystyle \hat\beta(\sigma)\) \(=\) \(\displaystyle (A'WA)^{-1}A'W\,b(\sigma)\) (9)

That uses up K directions of the M-dimensional moment space. What is left over has dimension M − K = 1, so the leftover moment vector is a scalar times a fixed direction:

\(\displaystyle e(\sigma)\) \(=\) \(\displaystyle s(\sigma)\) \(\displaystyle u\) \(,\qquad\) \(\displaystyle Q_c(\sigma)\) \(=\ N\,s(\sigma)^2\) (10)

Intuition. For each σ, run the linear IV regression of δ̃(σ) on the characteristics. It has one more instrument than regressors, so it has one overidentifying restriction. Qc(σ) is that regression's Sargan-type statistic. BLP searches for the σ at which the extra instrument is exactly uncorrelated with the residual.

The first-order condition for σ is

\(\displaystyle Q_c'(\sigma)\) \(=\ 2N\) \(\displaystyle s(\sigma)\) \(\displaystyle s'(\sigma)\) \(=\ 0\) (11)

so the optimizer can stop for two different reasons:

  • A root, s(σ) = 0. All moments are zero. If s′ ≠ 0 there, the Jacobian has full rank and the usual standard errors are valid.
  • No root: s′(σ) = 0 while s(σ) ≠ 0. The leftover moment has stopped moving with σ before reaching zero. A small change in σ then moves the moments only in directions that β can undo, so the σ column of G is a linear combination of the β columns. The Jacobian is singular.

What Danny is referring to: the rank deficiency

Danny's note states the general version of equation (11). With M = P the Jacobian G is square. The first-order condition (6) says G′Wg = 0. If G were invertible, that would force g = 0. So at any interior stationary point where the criterion is positive, G must be singular:

\(M=P,\quad g\neq 0,\quad G'Wg=0\) \(\ \Longrightarrow\ \) \(\displaystyle \operatorname{rank}(G)\le P-1\) (12)
  • Where he sees it: Table 10 (three RCs, 7 moments, 7 parameters) stops with rank 6 of 7 in both markets. The earlier fee-only model with 5 moments and 5 parameters is the same case, with rank at most 4 of 5.
  • What the missing direction is: a combination of σ and β that the instruments cannot tell apart. More dispersion in tastes, offset by a shift in mean tastes, leaves every moment unchanged.
  • Why it matters: the standard-error formula inverts G′WG, which does not exist. PyBLP then prints numbers from a generalized inverse. They are not valid standard errors. The paper's σ = 0.027 (0.013), with a covariance condition number of 2.4 × 10¹⁷, is an example.
  • What it is not: a PyBLP bug, or a general property of just-identified models. His note records a just-identified leveraged run that reaches a criterion of 10⁻²⁵ with full rank. The deficiency appears only when no root exists.

5. What moves s(σ): the instrument must have a first stage for a second regressor

Expanding the share inversion to second order in σ (the Salanié–Wolak approximation) turns the model into a linear regression with one extra regressor:

\(\displaystyle \ln\frac{s_{jt}}{s_{0t}}\) \(=\) \(\displaystyle x_{jt}'\beta\) \(+\) \(\displaystyle \sigma^2\) \(\displaystyle K_{jt}\) \(+\ \eta_j+\tau_t+\xi_{jt}\) \(,\qquad\) \(\displaystyle K_{jt}\) \(=\ p_{jt}\Big(\dfrac{p_{jt}}{2}-\) \(\displaystyle e_t\) \(\Big)\) (13)

To this order, BLP with one random coefficient is a linear IV regression with two endogenous regressors: the fee and K. The leftover moment becomes

\(\displaystyle s(\sigma)\) \(\approx\) \(\displaystyle s(0)\) \(-\ \sigma^2\) \(\displaystyle c\) \(\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\) \(\displaystyle \hat\sigma^2=\frac{s(0)}{c}\) (14)
  • Weak first stage (c near zero): the profile is flat. σ runs to implausible values or has no root. Its standard error is huge even when a root exists.
  • Wrong sign (s(0)/c below zero): the data ask for a negative variance. The minimum sits at σ = 0 with a positive criterion.
  • Strong and right-signed: a regular root.

Why estimates near zero are suspect. A normal taste shock is symmetric, so δ(σ) = δ(−σ) and s′(0) = 0 always. The point σ = 0 is a stationary point with a singular Jacobian in every data set. An estimate such as 0.027 with a non-zero criterion is most likely this point, not a small, precisely estimated dispersion.

What variation is in K. The fee slope implied by (13) is

\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial}{\partial p_{jt}}\big(\alpha p_{jt}+\sigma^2K_{jt}\big)\) \(=\) \(\displaystyle \alpha\) \(+\ \sigma^2\) \(\displaystyle (p_{jt}-e_t)\) (15)

A random coefficient therefore says the same thing as our cheap interaction: cheap funds face steeper demand. It imposes a slope that is linear in the fee, where the cheap dummy is a step. With fund fixed effects and fees that rarely change, the within-fund part of K is roughly

\(\displaystyle \tilde K_{jt}\) \(\approx\) \(\displaystyle \bar p_j\,\tilde p_{jt}\) \(-\) \(\displaystyle \bar p_j\,\tilde e_t\) (16)

This is why σ stopped being identified once fee × cheap entered our model: the two terms compete for the same variation. The expansion is rough when σ·p is large, so read it as a guide to the mechanism.

6. What an extra instrument does, and what it does not

What Danny is referring to: the extra IV

With one more excluded instrument, M = P + 1. The space of leftover moments now has dimension R + 1 instead of R. The stationarity condition still takes away one direction, but R remain, so the R random-coefficient columns can be independent even when the criterion is positive. The rank obstacle in (12) is gone.

The new instrument must also see the missing direction. Let \(v\) be the direction the old moments could not detect and \(q_v\) the change in the demand shock along it. Rank is restored exactly when

\(\displaystyle \tilde z_+'\) \(\displaystyle q_v\) \(/N\ \neq\ 0\) (17)

In his runs this holds: the rank goes from 6 of 7 to 7 of 7 in both markets.

Full rank is a low bar. Almost any instrument that is not a linear combination of the old ones satisfies (17) numerically. What matters is how strongly it does, and the note's own numbers answer that:

  • Passive funds: rank is restored but σ on the fee is still unidentified in any useful sense. The elasticities do not move because the estimate is still close to σ = 0.
  • Leveraged funds: the fee dispersion is identified with or without the fifth instrument.
  • A separate warning sign: the four-IV fits end at criteria of 43.1 and 115.9 with zero degrees of freedom. If these are on the J-statistic scale, the moment conditions cannot all hold at any parameter value. That points to an invalid instrument or a misspecified model, and adding a moment does not fix it.

7. Which instruments should identify the random coefficient on the expense ratio

The requirement from Section 5 is a strong first stage for K̃, beyond the first stage for the fee itself. Two kinds of variation deliver it: fee changes that differ along the fee distribution, and changes in which rivals sit close to a fund in fee.

What will not help.

  • More instruments that only shift the fee level, such as further Hausman variants. They strengthen the first stage for the fee, not for K.
  • Counts of entrants that ignore fee distance. They identify σ only through an accidental correlation with K.
  • The second term of (16) on its own. It is a trend by fee level, and any trend in demand toward cheap funds would be read as taste dispersion.

Checks before trusting any σ.

  1. Run the linear version (13) by IV. Report the conditional first-stage F for K̃ and the sign of σ².
  2. Plot the concentrated criterion over a grid of σ. A regular estimate shows a clear interior minimum at or near zero criterion.
  3. Report the smallest singular value of the scaled Jacobian, not just its rank.
  4. With over-identification, report the J test and the share of fund-months with upward-sloping demand.

8. Where this leaves the paper

For passive funds, the heterogeneity in fee sensitivity is real, and equation (15) shows it is the same heterogeneity our fee × cheap interaction measures. The interaction has a first stage we can verify (F of 167). The random coefficient asks the same instruments for a second first stage that they do not have. Unless one of the instruments in Section 7 delivers that, the logit IV with the interaction is the specification the data can support, with the one-RC model reported as a sensitivity check.

9. The two parts of K: two experiments behind one coefficient

Equation (16) splits the within-fund variation in K into two terms. Each is a different experiment, and each needs a different kind of instrument. The random coefficient forces a single coefficient, σ², on both.

\(\displaystyle \tilde K_{jt}\) \(\approx\) \(\displaystyle \underbrace{\bar p_j\,\tilde p_{jt}}_{\text{own fee}}\) \(-\) \(\displaystyle \underbrace{\bar p_j\,\tilde e_t}_{\text{market position}}\) (16)

  • The own-fee term agrees with the cheap interaction. Entered alone, it says demand is steeper for funds with lower fees, which is the direction σ above zero implies. For reference, fee × cheap is −22.7 (0.8), and −42.0 (5.7) with month × cheap fixed effects.
  • The full K has the opposite sign with every instrument set. The market-position term drives this. The market mean fee et fell from 0.19 in 2010 to 0.08 in 2024. A positive σ² predicts that expensive funds hold up better as that happens. The negative coefficient says they did worse than their own fees and the fixed effects explain.
  • One coefficient cannot fit both. The random coefficient ties the two terms to the same σ². The data want opposite signs. In the linear version, passive funds are therefore in the wrong-sign case of Section 4, where a normal random coefficient stops at σ = 0 with a positive criterion. The paper's 0.027 is close to zero. So are Danny's 0.049 and 0.040 in his five-instrument fits that add an age random coefficient (Tables 8 and 10). His fee-only fit (Table 7) gives 1.875 (0.367); Section 10 shows why that value is fragile.
  • What the second term does not show. Here it is identified from one market-wide time series interacted with the fee level. That cannot separate substitution patterns from a secular move toward cheap funds. A clean test of the rival-position channel needs variation in rival positions across funds within the same month, which is what near-versus-far entry and exit provides.
  • Entering the two terms separately does not settle it. With three instruments the estimates are −92.5 (36.1) on p²/2 and −69.1 (16.1) on −p·et, but the first of these is +99 when it enters alone. Both terms are highly correlated with the fee itself (0.77 for K̃).
